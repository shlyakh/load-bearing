Receipts for forecasting papers · a transfer test · updated 14 Nov 2026

A model and a regression tied in Britain. Do they tie on a US grid?

A study published this week found that a pretrained forecasting model and a plain regression roughly tie on Great Britain's electricity demand once both get the same weather. We asked the same question of two zones of PJM, a US grid operator, forecasting a full week ahead. Then we checked the answer three ways: against the field's standard benchmark, with better weather forecasts, and on two fresh zones.

Background

Electricity demand is forecast before the power is made.

Power cannot be stored cheaply at grid scale, so supply must match demand every hour. Grid operators decide days ahead which power plants will run. They decide from a forecast of demand, called load.

Seven regional operators run the wholesale grid for most of the United States. PJM is the largest, with about 65 million people across 13 states and Washington DC.

A forecast that is too low means expensive last-minute power. One that is too high means plants paid to stand by.

What this establishes

Load forecasts decide which plants are scheduled, days ahead. A few percent of error is real money, which is why each new forecasting model gets attention.

The benchmark

Two PJM zones that behave differently.

PJM is split into about twenty delivery zones. The benchmark forecasts two of them, one hour at a time, a full week ahead. JC is Jersey Central in New Jersey: residential, and driven by summer heat. DOM is Dominion in Virginia: large and growing, with heavy data-centre demand.

Temperature is the main driver in both, so every method gets the same temperature series: the average of three weather points per zone. A weather point is a place we picked inside the zone, read from a weather model's grid. It is not a weather station, and no station readings are used. Both zones have public hourly data back to 2019 and sit in different climates 400 km apart.

Two more zones, AEP and AE, were added later for a fresh test. Chapter 9 covers them.

Try this

Switch the bars to "Multiple of own average". In megawatts DOM dwarfs JC. Measured against its own average, JC's peak is the larger of the two. A method that wins in one zone and not the other has a win that depends on the zone.

The methods

Eight methods, the same history and the same temperature.

Every Monday at midnight each method forecasts the next 168 hours. Nothing after that moment may be used. A week ahead is hard because the weather forecast gets worse each day out, and because holidays and heat waves break the weekly rhythm.

Error is the average gap in megawatts between forecast and measured load over the 168 hours, averaged over 48 test weeks. Two methods are compared by dividing one error by the other. A ratio of 0.73 means 27% less error than the regression with temperature.

The regression with temperature is the first baseline, the temperature-only regression. It was chosen from one recent study and fixed before scoring. The Vanilla benchmark, the field's standard, was added afterwards as a secondary row (chapter 7).

What this establishes

Chapters 3 to 6 score every method as a ratio to one baseline, the regression with temperature, on the same weeks and the same inputs. That baseline was named before any scoring. Chapters 7 to 9 change the baseline to the field's standard.

The receipt · protocol-v1

Against the first regression, the tie does not transfer to DOM and is undecided on JC.

The Great Britain study forecast one day ahead over 960 days. With the same weather given to both, Chronos-2's error was 0.966 of the regression's, and the 90% range reached 1.

The rule for reading our result was fixed before scoring. If the whole 90% range sits below 0.90, the model is worth switching to. If the whole range sits between 0.90 and 1.10, the two are a practical tie and the claim transfers. Anything else is undecided.

Keep the two studies separate

This is a transfer test and says nothing about whether the Great Britain result is correct. That study is day ahead, and its regression also had a 2-day lag, wind and solar. Ours is a week ahead with a 7-day lag and temperature only. Our regression is also not the strongest simple method: LightGBM on the same inputs scores 0.857 on JC and 0.947 on DOM, and the field's standard benchmark closes most of the gap (chapter 7).

Inside the week

The lead is largest on day 1, and it depends on temperature.

The week-ahead ratio averages seven forecast days. Day 1 is the closest match to the Great Britain study's day-ahead setting, and there the model leads in both zones. On JC the ranges for days 3 to 5 include 1.

Two checks test where the lead comes from. In the scored run the model sees load up to the cutoff, while real load data arrives 1 to 2 days late, so one row withholds the last 48 hours. Another row gives Chronos-2 no weather at all.

What this establishes

A realistic data delay shrinks the day-1 lead and leaves the week-ahead verdicts unchanged. Without temperature, Chronos-2 is worse than the regression with temperature on DOM and no better on JC. Its lead comes from using temperature well, more than from the load history alone.

Out-of-sample check

Timestral has the lowest error and one unseen week.

A pretrained model may have been trained on the public series it is later tested on. The check is a date: only weeks that start after the model's weights were published count as unseen. Earlier years of the same series may still be in the training data of any of these models.

Timestral with temperature has the lowest error on the board in every zone and weather setting. Its only weights file is dated 15 Oct 2026, after all but one of the 48 scored weeks began. The Great Britain study also treats Timestral as having no usable unseen backtest.

What this establishes

Timestral's score cannot be told apart from leakage with this window, so it is shown and marked as not out-of-sample. The ratio on weeks after the weights would rest on a single week and is not reported.

A second check · protocol-v1.1, secondary

Against the field's standard benchmark, the lead largely disappears.

After scoring, we searched review papers and forecasting competitions for the load-forecasting field's own standard baseline. It is Tao's Vanilla Benchmark: no lagged load, a curved temperature response that varies by month and hour, two years of history, plus a trend. It was added in two forms, with and without the trend, and scored once.

Vanilla with trend has 23% less error than our first regression on DOM and 18% less on JC. Against it, Chronos-2's lead is gone on JC and small on DOM.

What this establishes

The verdicts in chapter 4 stand, because their baseline was fixed in advance. What changes is how much they can bear: Chronos-2 beats our first regression, not a standard load-forecasting regression. Against the standard one, the tie reported for Great Britain largely reappears: a practical tie on JC, inconclusive on DOM.

Weather sources · protocol-v1.2, secondary

As the weather forecast gets better, the regression catches up.

Every model gets the same temperature. Here it comes from four sources: the GFS/HRRR mix used in the main results, ECMWF IFS at 0.25°, ECMWF AIFS, and ERA5. ERA5 is the reference record of past weather, which we treat as the true temperature from here on; using it for the forecast week gives perfect weather.

A correction came with it. The series the earlier results called actual weather was an ECMWF IFS analysis, not ERA5 reanalysis. This page now calls it analysed weather.

What this establishes

Against the Vanilla benchmark with trend, Chronos-2 is level under every source (0.94 to 1.04). On DOM the ratio climbs as the weather improves. AIFS gives the lowest load error for every model; IFS was no better than the GFS/HRRR mix for load this year. ERA5 is a model product, not station readings, and AIFS was trained on it, so an ERA5 truth may favour AIFS.

The fresh test · protocol-v2

On two zones no model had been run on, Chronos-2 has no clear lead.

Every model had already been seen on DOM and JC, and the first baseline turned out weaker than the field's standard. So the comparison, the baseline, the weather source and two new zones were fixed in advance, and the scored weeks were run once. The comparison is Chronos-2 with temperature against the Vanilla benchmark with trend, with ECMWF AIFS forecast weather.

The zones were picked by a written rule, as the closest matches on load statistics from before the test year. AEP, American Electric Power, from Indiana to Virginia, matches DOM: as large, and growing. AE, Atlantic City Electric in southern New Jersey, matches JC: strongly summer-peaking. AE borders JC and their daily load moves closely together. The rule was written with all 20 zones' load statistics in view, but before any model ran on a new zone.

For these zones the weather points are the weather-model grid cells that hold the airports PJM uses, averaged with PJM's weights: five for AEP, one for AE.

What this establishes

On the fresh zones both readings are inconclusive: neither clears the 10% bar, and neither is a confirmed tie. The calendar made Chronos-2 worse in all four zones, and holiday terms left the Vanilla benchmark within 3%. Lowest error on AEP is a competition-grade LightGBM; that comparison is also inconclusive.

Conclusion

What the evidence supports.

Under the first protocol, frozen before scoring, the rough tie reported for Great Britain does not transfer to DOM, where Chronos-2 with temperature clears the 10% bar against the temperature-only regression. On JC the range crosses the bar, so 48 weeks cannot separate a tie from a real difference.

That baseline was weaker than the field's standard. Against the Vanilla benchmark with trend the lead largely disappears, and on DOM it shrinks further as the weather forecast improves. On two fresh zones, fixed in advance, the comparison reads inconclusive on both.

The results have limits. The models saw load up to the cutoff, one year of weekly origins gives wide ranges, ERA5 is a model product rather than observations, and AE rests on a single grid cell.

One year of past weeks cannot settle it. The next test runs forward in time.
