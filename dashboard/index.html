The grid

Electricity has to be forecast before it is made

Power cannot be stored cheaply at grid scale, so supply must match demand every hour. Grid operators decide days ahead which power plants will run. They decide from a forecast of demand, called load.

Seven regional operators run the wholesale grid for about two thirds of the country's electricity customers. Elsewhere, in the West and the Southeast, a large utility or a federal agency runs each area, such as BPA, TVA, Southern and Duke. PJM is the largest operator: about 65 million people across 13 states and Washington DC.

A forecast that is too low means expensive last-minute power. One that is too high means plants paid to stand by. A few percent of error is real money, which is why every new forecasting model gets attention.

Map shaded by state for readability. Darker states belong to one of the seven regional operators, named in bold. Lighter states have none: the label names the largest grid operator in the state, and pointing at a state lists the main ones. Northwest, Southwest and Southeast, in italics, are the names EIA gives these regions; the dashed line separates the Northwest from the Southwest. Real territories cut across state lines: PJM also covers parts of Illinois, Indiana, Michigan, Kentucky, North Carolina and Tennessee.

Our benchmark

Two PJM zones that behave differently

PJM is split into about twenty delivery zones. We forecast two of them, one hour at a time, a full week ahead.

JC, Jersey Central, New Jersey. Average load; peak . Residential, driven by summer heat.
DOM, Dominion, Virginia. Average load; peak . Large and growing, with heavy data-centre demand.

Temperature is the main driver in both, so every model gets the same temperature: the average of three weather points per zone (the dots). A weather point is a place we picked inside the zone, read from the weather model's grid; it is not a weather station. We test with the forecast that was available at the time, and with weather analysed after the fact. The Weather tab adds ERA5, the reference record of past weather, and two more forecast sources.

The newest test: two new zones. Every model had already been seen on JC and DOM, so the latest version of the test (the New zones tab) was read on two zones no model had been run on. They were picked by a written rule, as the closest matches on load statistics from before the test year: AEP (, Indiana to Virginia) for DOM, at MW average and growing, and AE (, southern New Jersey) for JC, at MW with a summer peak times its winter peak.

JC and DOM, the original benchmark. AEP and AE, the new test. Shaded areas are the zones; dots are weather points. For AEP and AE the points are the weather-model grid cells that hold the airports PJM uses for those zones, averaged with PJM's weights.

Load: EIA hourly data. Weather: Open-Meteo. Zone areas are drawn by county, from EIA's list of the counties each zone's main utility serves. A county shared by two utilities is drawn for one, and co-operatives and city utilities inside a zone are not mapped separately.

The methods

What is being compared, in plain terms

How error is measured

For each of 48 test weeks, the average gap in megawatts between forecast and actual over the 168 hours.

How two methods are compared

One method's average error divided by the other's. 0.73 means 27% less error.

How sure we are

The 48 weeks are reshuffled 2,000 times to see how far the ratio moves by chance. The range shown holds 90% of those results.

What counts as a win

Fixed before scoring: the whole range must be more than 10% better. Otherwise it is a tie or undecided.

The receipt

A study said the model and the regression tie. Does that hold here?

A pre-registered study of Great Britain's demand, published this week, found that once both get the same weather, Chronos-2's error is 0.97 of a regression's, a rough tie. We ran the same question on PJM, a week ahead, on weeks the model cannot have seen in training.

Error ratio: the model's average error divided by the comparison regression's, on a log scale. Below 1 the model is better. The shaded band is our 10% bar, fixed before scoring: inside it the two are a practical tie. Bars are 90% ranges, but over different samples: the claim's over the GB study's own 960 days (day-ahead), ours over our whole weeks, so the narrower claimed bar reflects more data, not a stronger result. The third row, against the field's standard benchmark, is secondary and does not change the verdict. Source of the claim: Zenodo record 24161799.

A second check: against the field's standard benchmark

After scoring, we searched review papers and forecasting competitions for the load-forecasting field's own standard baseline. It is a different regression, known as Tao's Vanilla Benchmark: no lagged load, a curved temperature response that varies by month and hour, two years of history, plus a trend. It beats our original regression by about 20% in both zones.

Read with care

  • ERA5 is the temperature truth here. It is a weather model's reconstruction of the past, not station readings. AIFS was trained on ERA5, so measuring against ERA5 may favour AIFS.
  • IFS 0.25° was no better than the GFS/HRRR mix for load over this year.
  • AIFS covers fewer weeks. Its archive is incomplete for three April weeks, so its column has weeks and the others . Its error levels are not strictly like-for-like; each ratio is paired within its own weeks.
  • A correction. The series these results first called "actual weather" was an ECMWF IFS analysis, not ERA5 reanalysis. The Leaderboard now calls it analysed weather.
How the processing runs

Set up once, then three agents turn each week's claims into receipts

The benchmark is built and frozen once. After that, each week's run goes through the same five stages. Three of them are agents: a language model following written instructions, with a fixed list of tools. Each agent can do its web work in two ways, with Claude's own search or with You.com, and the outputs are kept apart so the two can be compared.

Once

Set up the benchmark

  1. DataLoad and weather

    Hourly load for four PJM zones and the temperature each forecaster would have had. Every raw file is hashed; scoring refuses to run if a file changed.

    fetch.py
  2. RulesFreeze the protocol

    Forecast every Monday at midnight for the next 168 hours. Weeks, baseline, the 10% bar and the verdict wording are fixed at a git tag.

    protocol.yaml
  3. Dry runGate weeks

    Code is debugged only on a few earlier weeks, never on the scored ones.

    harness/
  4. ScoreScore once

    Baselines and pretrained models each run once on the scored weeks. Weights are pinned to a dated revision.

    results/leaderboard
  5. TargetWrite the problem spec

    What counts as relevant to our problem, and what each label means. The judge scores against this file.

    spec.yaml
Every week

From new claims to receipts

Today a person starts the run with one command (tools/pipeline.py) and the stages run in the background, each one recorded in runs/. A weekly schedule is planned, not built.

  1. Stage 1 · no agentRead the listingstools/feeds.py

    A plain script reads public listings directly. They give exact dates and a complete list for each site, which web search does not.

    Writes candidates/feeds/: title, URL and date only. Abstracts stay on the laptop.

  2. Stage 2 · agent 1Scan: find the claims/scan

    Shortlists listing items by title, then runs each query once per site inside the date window. Drops pages that are undated, off topic, duplicates or rewrites, and keeps claims that point at a primary source, closest to our problem first.

    With Claude's own search
    • Web search for each query and site group
    • Web fetch to confirm a date or a source
    With You.com
    • Search, with an exact date range and one site: per query; every result comes back dated
    • Contents to read a page

    Writes candidates/<provider>/ and a ledger with the reason every page was kept or dropped.

    Then a plain script (tools/merge_candidates.py) combines the scans into one list, folds duplicates, and marks who found each claim. The judge works from that list, best first.

  3. Stage 3 · agent 2Judge: check one claim/judge

    Reads the primary sources, states the claim with a citation for every statement, and checks what decides whether it applies to us: was the test planned in advance, did the baseline get the same inputs, how far ahead, what licence, could the model have trained on our test weeks. Then it gives one of four labels.

    With Claude's own search
    • A research helper that searches and fetches
    • Web fetch for each primary source
    With You.com
    • Research: one task that returns an answer with its sources as a list
    • Contents: about eight pages per claim
    • Search: up to two targeted lookups

    Writes scorecards/<provider>/, checked against a fixed schema, and evidence/<provider>/ with one row per source. No primary source, no pass.

  4. Stage 4 · agent 3Run: measure it/run-candidate

    Only for claims labelled "can be run now". No web search. What happens depends on the model the claim needs:

    Already on the leaderboard
    • Go straight to the receipt
    New model the library wraps
    • Pin the weights, run the gate weeks, then the scored weeks once
    Needs new code
    • Stop and report what a person has to write

    A background run may only take the first path. It never downloads weights, changes code or starts a model by itself.

    Writes forecasts and a leaderboard row under results/, when a model is run.

  5. Stage 5 · no agentWrite the receipttools/receipt.py

    A plain script fills in the receipt from the scored results. The agent supplies the claim in its own words and how the claim's setup differs from ours; it never supplies a number.

    Writes the receipt into the scorecard: claimed, measured ratio with its range per zone, the setup mismatch, weeks the model cannot have seen, and the verdict. This page is rebuilt from those files.

Scoring

Two scores: a label for the claim, a ratio for the model

1. The judge's label

Given by agent 2 from the sources alone, before anything is run.

LabelMeaning
Can be run nowRelevant, has a primary source, and the model fits our benchmark as it is.
Needs more work to runRelevant and sourced, but running it takes more than an adapter.
Thin evidenceRelevant, but the primary source is missing or weak. A claim with no primary source cannot score higher.
Off targetNot about our problem, or nothing usable behind the claim.

2. The measured ratio

Each week's error is the average miss over its 168 hours, in megawatts. The ratio is the model's average weekly error divided by the baseline's: 0.90 means 10% less error. The range comes from re-drawing whole weeks 2,000 times, the same weeks for both.

The reading depends on where the whole range falls against the 10% bar:

Chronos-2 with temperature in every row. Dot: the ratio. Line: its 90% range. Shaded: 0.90 to 1.10, a practical tie if the whole range is inside.

3. From the reading to the receipt's wording

The receipt is a transfer test: does the published result carry over to our problem? So the wording depends on what was claimed.

What we measuredIf the claim was "about a tie"If the claim was "the model is better"
Model clearly better (range below 0.90)Does not transferTransfers
Practical tie (range inside 0.90 to 1.10)TransfersDoes not transfer
Baseline clearly better (range above 1.10)Does not transferDoes not transfer
Inconclusive (range crosses a bar)PartiallyPartially

Zones that disagree give "partially". If most scored weeks fall before the model's weights were published, the receipt says "untestable": the model may have trained on them.

Why trust it

The rules were fixed before the scores existed

  • Frozen protocol. Weeks, baseline, the 10% bar and the wording of each verdict were tagged before any scored run.
  • Scored once. Each model ran once on the scored weeks; debugging used separate earlier weeks.
  • Same inputs. Every model sees the same load history and the same temperature.
  • Unseen weeks stated. Each row says how many weeks start after the model's weights were published.
  • A transfer test, not a verdict on the study. The study forecast one day ahead with more weather inputs; we forecast a week ahead with temperature only.
